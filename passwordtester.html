Password Security

Why Password Strength is Critical

Alarming Statistics:
  • 81% of hacking-related corporate breaches stem from weak or reused passwords
  • 94% of passwords are reused or weak, leaving users vulnerable to attacks
  • 70% of weak passwords can be cracked in less than 1 second
  • 38% of all cyberattacks involve credential abuse or phishing

Passwords as Your First Line of Defense

In today's digital landscape, where cyber threats are increasingly sophisticated, strong passwords serve as the first line of defense against unauthorized access. Despite advances in biometric authentication and other security technologies, passwords remain the primary method for protecting our digital accounts.

The High Cost of Poor Password Security

Data breaches cost over $4 million per incident on average, and password-related incidents can lead to:

  • Identity Theft: Weak passwords make it easier for hackers to gain unauthorized access to accounts to steal sensitive personal information
  • Financial Loss: Four AustralianSuper members lost a combined $500,000 in credential stuffing attacks
  • Account Hijacking: Cybercriminals often use weak passwords to take over social media, email, and other personal accounts
  • Business Disruption: In 2021, the Colonial Pipeline experienced a significant ransomware attack that disrupted fuel supplies across the Eastern United States. The breach was traced back to a single compromised password

Modern Password Threats

Common Attack Methods:
  • Credential Stuffing: Automated tools test stolen username and password combinations across multiple websites with success rates between 0.2% and 2.0%
  • Brute Force Attacks: Effective 20% of the time, where bad actors randomly guess passwords
  • Dictionary Attacks: Using common passwords and word combinations
  • Social Engineering: 63% of social engineering attacks involve compromised login credentials
Key Takeaway: Length is a simple but powerful defense against brute-force attacks. Recent guidance from cybersecurity authorities emphasizes the need for longer passwords, with CISA recommending at least 16 characters.

What Makes a Strong Password?

  • Length: At least 16 characters (CISA recommendation) or 15 characters (NIST recommendation)
  • Complexity: Mix of uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols
  • Unpredictability: Avoid personal information, dictionary words, and common patterns
  • Uniqueness: Different password for each account

Strong Password Examples

Method 1: Random Complex Passwords

Use a random string of mixed-case letters, numbers and symbols:

K9$mR7@nP4&jL2#qW8
Zx9!Lm3@Qs7$Rb2*Nk5
P7&Gj4#Mp9$Vt1@Xc6!

Method 2: Passphrases (Recommended)

Create a memorable phrase of 4–7 unrelated words. This is called a "passphrase":

coffee dragon bicycle telescope
mountain keyboard galaxy sandwich
purple elephant jazz harmonica waterfall

Method 3: Enhanced Passphrases

Passphrases with numbers, symbols, and mixed case for extra security:

Coffee2Dragon!Bicycle7Telescope
Mountain$9Keyboard#Galaxy3Sandwich
Purple4!Elephant@Jazz2*Harmonica&Waterfall

Method 4: Sentence-Based Passwords

Use sentences or phrases. For example, "breadandbutteryum". Some systems will even let you use spaces: "bread and butter yum":

I love hiking in the mountains on Sundays!
My favorite coffee shop opens at 7am daily
Reading 3 books per month keeps me happy

Password Creation Tips

  • Don't use information in your password that others might know about you or that's in your social media (e.g. birthdays, children's or pet's names, car model, etc.)
  • Avoid simple words, phrases, and patterns that are easy to guess
  • Instead of enforcing overly rigid password complexity requirements, encourage the use of passphrases like "CyberStrong2025!" that are easier to remember and harder to guess
  • Use a unique password for each website. If crooks steal your account information from one site, they'll try to use those credentials on hundreds of other well-known websites
Length vs. Complexity: Passwords with 10 characters from a set of 94 possible characters yield ~53.9 quintillion possible combinations. Adding just one extra character increases the combinations to ~5 sextillion and makes brute-force attacks exponentially more difficult.

Vulnerabilities of Weak Passwords

Most Common Weak Passwords

The top five stolen passwords in 2025 were '123456', 'admin', '12345678', 'password', and 'Password'. These passwords can be cracked almost instantly.

Critical Vulnerabilities

  • Short Passwords: Most people use 8–10 character passwords (42%), with eight being the most popular
  • Simple Patterns: 27% of passwords consist of only lowercase letters and digits
  • Password Reuse: 52% of people reuse the same password across multiple accounts
  • Personal Information: Using names, birthdays, pet names, or other easily discoverable information
  • Dictionary Words: Hackers will use dictionaries of words and commonly used passwords to guess your password

How Weak Passwords Are Exploited

1. Brute Force Attacks

70% of weak passwords can be cracked in less than 1 second by hackers using simple brute force attacks. Attackers use automated tools to systematically guess passwords.

2. Dictionary Attacks

Attackers use lists of common passwords, words, and phrases. Even if you follow password complexity guidelines, you're still not necessarily safe. 230 million stolen passwords met standard complexity requirements, including things like 'P@ssw0rd'.

3. Credential Stuffing

If crooks steal your account information from one site, they'll try to use those credentials on hundreds of other well-known websites, such as banking, social media, or online shopping, hoping you've reused the password elsewhere.

4. Social Engineering

Attackers gather personal information from social media and other sources to guess passwords. 'Patches2022!' won't work because it's a predictable pattern that people can deduce from your social media information.

Real-World Impact

Recent Breaches in 2025:
  • Malware attacks resulted in the theft of approximately one billion passwords in January 2025
  • Bank Sepah breach exposed over 42 million customer records due to password vulnerabilities
  • Australian superannuation funds targeted through credential stuffing attacks

The Password Reuse Problem

Password reuse is a common problem and according to a Google/Harris survey, 52% of people reuse the same password across multiple accounts. This risky behaviour has led to a huge surge in credential stuffing attacks.

Protection Strategies

  • Use Password Managers: A password manager is an easy-to-use program that generates, stores and even fills in all your passwords
  • Enable Multi-Factor Authentication: According to Microsoft, accounts are more than 99.9% less likely to be compromised if MFA is enabled
  • Regular Monitoring: Check if your passwords have been compromised in data breaches
  • Education: Stay informed about current password security threats and best practices
